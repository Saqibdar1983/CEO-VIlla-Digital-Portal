Project Resumption & Information Traceability

Issue / Observation
Project resumed after a long hold period without a consolidated project status record. There was no clear data available for delivered items, pending materials, previous decisions, latest approved drawings, or outstanding procurement/design issues.
Root Cause
Project information was not maintained in one controlled, centralized system. Key records such as approvals, drawing revision history, minutes of meetings, comments, POs, delivery notes, and pending decisions were scattered across Oracle, emails, hard copies, and individual staff knowledge. Most of the original project team had already left the company.
Impact on Project
A significant amount of time and effort was required to reconstruct the project status through reverse engineering. POs from Oracle had to be checked against delivery notes item by item for sanitary items, air devices, AHUs, package units, VRF equipment, and other mechanical materials. This delayed procurement closeout, discrepancy reporting, new PR issuance, and overall project progress.
Lesson Learned
Project knowledge must not depend on individuals. For projects that are stopped, transferred, or restarted after a long gap, all procurement, design, approval, delivery, installation, and decision records must remain traceable through a controlled document management system and a live status tracker.
Recommended Preventive Action
Implement Aconex, or an equivalent controlled document management platform, as the mandatory single source of truth for approvals, drawings, comments, revision history, minutes of meetings, and technical decisions. Before any project hold or staff transfer, issue a formal project status freeze report covering latest drawings, approved materials, PO and delivery status, pending PRs/RFIs, discrepancies, installed items, and remaining scope. Maintain a live procurement-delivery-installation tracker linked to document references.
2

Concealed Underground Plumbing Documentation

Issue / Observation
Underground plumbing services were already concealed when the project was resumed, but their exact routing, depth, and connection points were not traceable. No site photographs, videos, redline drawings, or as-built drawings were available to confirm where the underground drainage/plumbing lines were passing.
Root Cause
Underground services were executed without proper progressive documentation. Redline drawings were not maintained during installation, photo/video records were not saved in a controlled folder, and as-built drawings were not prepared or updated before the project was put on hold. Knowledge of the concealed works remained with staff who later left the company.
Impact on Project
Manhole marking, excavation planning, and coordination with other underground services became difficult and risky. Additional time was spent making small trial openings to trace the lines, confirming manhole locations, and using mobile cameras after manhole completion to identify the routing and prepare the as-built information. This created delay, rework risk, and potential damage risk to existing concealed services.
Lesson Learned
Hidden underground services must be documented while they are being executed, not after they are covered. Redline drawings, site photographs, videos, inspection records, and confirmed levels/coordinates are essential for future tracing, coordination, safe excavation, and accurate as-built preparation, especially if the project is stopped or handed over to a new team.
Recommended Preventive Action
For every underground service, make redline drawings mandatory before backfilling or covering the work. Take clear photos and videos showing pipe routing, fittings, levels, slopes, sleeves, manholes, tie-in points, and reference dimensions from fixed grid lines/walls. Save all records in a permanent controlled folder/Aconex with date, area, system, and drawing reference. Conduct inspection and approval before backfilling, then update as-built drawings progressively instead of waiting until project completion.
3

Domestic Water Supply Redundancy & Contingency Configuration

Issue / Observation
The domestic water supply system for the CEO residence did not have a fully reliable contingency arrangement. Although two booster pumps, two hot water circulation pumps, and two water heaters were available, the system was not arranged smartly to maintain water supply in case one complete pumping set, control panel, filtration line, or related equipment failed. There was also no overhead water tank, and the complete building water supply depended on the basement pumping arrangement.
Root Cause
The original design and piping arrangement provided equipment redundancy but did not provide practical operational redundancy. The two systems were not sufficiently cross-connected, bypassed, or provided with clearly defined emergency operation modes. The failure scenarios, valve operation sequence, filtration bypass requirement, and emergency supply strategy were not fully considered during design coordination and construction.
Impact on Project
Any major failure in the booster system, irrigation/filtration connection, control panel, or pumping set could interrupt water supply to the residence. For a high-importance project and in Saudi climate conditions, interruption of domestic water supply creates serious operational risk, client dissatisfaction, and emergency maintenance pressure.
Lesson Learned
Redundancy is not only the availability of standby equipment; it must also be supported by practical piping arrangement, bypass lines, isolation valves, independent operation modes, and a clear contingency plan. Critical residential projects must be reviewed for real operational continuity, especially for domestic water supply, before final handover.
Recommended Preventive Action
Review critical utility systems during design and pre-handover stages for failure scenarios and emergency operation. Provide cross-connections, bypass arrangements, isolation valves, valve tags, and clear operation procedures where required. For this project, the pump room piping was rebuilt so that booster and irrigation pumping systems can operate with or without filtration, take water directly from the tank when required, and temporarily support each other during repair. A contingency plan with eight operating modes and valve tags was prepared to guide the FM/operator on how to maintain water supply according to the actual fault condition. For future projects, such contingency modes should be included in design review, shop drawings, testing/commissioning, O&M manuals, and operator training.
4

Remote Monitoring & Early Warning Alarms for Critical Pits

Issue / Observation
For a residence villa where the CEO’s family will live, access for maintenance staff is limited during family hours, but the MEP systems are spread across the villa. Critical water and sewage levels, including the domestic water tank, main sewage tank, basement sump pits, and rainwater pits, could not be monitored remotely. Although each pit was provided with duplex pumps, there was no level indication or alarm system to warn the technician if the water level became high or if a pump failed.
Root Cause
The design did not include sufficient remote monitoring, local alarms, BMS integration, or clear level indication for critical tanks and pits. The system depended mainly on visual inspection, which is not practical for a high-security/private residence with restricted access. The main sewage tank is connected to the municipality line; however, a recent municipality line blockage highlighted the risk of backflow, overflow, and delayed fault detection.
Impact on Project
High water or sewage levels may go unnoticed until overflow occurs, especially when technicians cannot freely access the villa for frequent inspection. This can cause water damage, sewage backup, unpleasant smell, hygiene concerns, emergency maintenance, disruption to the residents, and serious client dissatisfaction. Reliance on visual inspection every few days is not reliable for such a critical residence.
Lesson Learned
For high-end residential projects, critical water and sewage systems must be designed with remote monitoring and early warning provisions. Duplex pumps alone are not enough if there is no alarm, status indication, or BMS notification. Hidden or restricted-access systems must be monitored from an accessible technical area such as the pump room or through BMS, with clear alarm logic and response procedures.
Recommended Preventive Action
Provide level indicators and high-level alarms for domestic water tanks, sewage tanks, basement sump pits, and rainwater pits. Connect critical alarms, pump run/trip status, and tank/pit levels to the BMS or provide a dedicated alarm panel inside the pump room. For this project, a transparent pipe level indicator was provided in the pump room for the domestic water supply tank, and backflow preventers were installed to reduce reverse-flow risk in case of manhole surcharging. Until full monitoring is available, strict preventive maintenance and scheduled visual inspection must be followed. For future projects, basement sump pits should be interconnected below raft level through a controlled equalizing/overflow pipe with isolation valves, so that in case one pit or pump set fails, overflow can be diverted to another pit and the other duplex pump set can temporarily support the faulty pit.

Spatial Coordination of Duct Accessories with Ceiling Constraints

Issue / Observation
Actual dimensions of duct accessories were not properly coordinated with the available ceiling space and final installation requirements. Round VCDs installed during the earlier project stage were approximately 250–350 mm long, while the drawing symbol showed only a short indicative length of about 100 mm. The drawings showed adequate space for linear plenum boxes and flexible ducts, but actual site conditions provided less than the required space in several locations.
Root Cause
The coordination drawings used symbolic accessory representation instead of actual approved product dimensions. The physical dimensions of VCDs, plenum boxes, flexible ducts, branch ducts, insulation, ceiling clearance, and access requirements were not verified together before procurement and installation. The accessories were installed in 2019–2020, before the final installation constraints were fully reviewed.
Impact on Project
There was insufficient space to install plenum boxes and flexible ducts as planned. Existing long VCDs had to be removed and replaced with customized short dampers of approximately 100 mm length. Ducts had to be pushed back toward the wall, and in some areas branch ducts were raised or modified to create space for plenum boxes. This caused rework, material wastage, additional manpower, delay, and coordination pressure during the final stage of the project.
Lesson Learned
Duct accessories must not be treated only as drawing symbols. Actual manufacturer dimensions, installation clearances, access space, and ceiling coordination must be verified before procurement and installation. For congested ceiling areas, the complete assembly—branch duct, VCD, flexible duct, plenum box, grille/diffuser, insulation, support, and access requirement—must be checked as one coordinated installation.
Recommended Preventive Action
During shop drawing and material submittal review, include actual dimensional details for VCDs, plenum boxes, flexible ducts, grilles/diffusers, and all duct accessories. Prepare a typical coordinated installation detail for congested ceiling areas and verify it with architectural ceiling levels, structural beams, other MEP services, and access panel locations. Before bulk procurement or installation, conduct a mock-up or first-fix sample inspection to confirm available space. Where space is limited, specify short-body dampers or customized accessories before installation, instead of modifying completed work later. Maintain a coordination checklist for duct accessories covering actual length, service clearance, access, and maintainability.

HVAC Equipment Scope Package & Filter Access Verification

Issue / Observation
Rooftop package units were approved, procured, foundations were cast, and units were installed before confirming that required air filters were supplied as part of the unit package. After reviewing the catalogue, it was found that the internal slot shown in the unit was not for filters and that filters had to be purchased separately. No clear note was included in the PO or material submittal highlighting that filters were excluded.
Root Cause
Material submittal, PO, and catalogue review did not clearly identify unit accessories, exclusions, and required loose items. The design/approval process focused mainly on the unit model and physical dimensions, while accessories such as filters, filter racks, access provisions, and maintenance requirements were not fully checked before foundation casting and installation.
Impact on Project
Additional site modification was required after the rooftop units were already installed. Return air ductwork had to be cut and modified, filter access provisions and sliding assemblies had to be fabricated, and customized filters had to be installed. This caused loss of time, extra manpower, additional material cost, and rework risk near completed installation areas.
Lesson Learned
Approval of mechanical equipment must include verification of the complete supply package, not only the unit model. For AHUs, FAHUs, package units, and similar equipment, all required accessories, filters, filter racks, control items, sensors, dampers, isolators, drain connections, access clearances, and maintenance requirements must be confirmed before procurement, foundation works, and installation.
Recommended Preventive Action
Prepare a mandatory equipment/accessory checklist during material submittal and procurement review. The checklist should confirm supplied items, excluded items, loose accessories, filter type and size, spare filter requirement, access clearance, and maintenance method. Catalogue exclusions must be highlighted in the submittal, PO, and technical comparison. Before casting foundations or installing units, conduct a final constructability review using actual catalogue data. Record final filter sizes and details in the O&M manual for future replacement.

Project-Wide Location Identification & Room Numbering System

Issue / Observation
Architectural drawings provided room names, but proper room numbers or unique location identification codes were not available for all rooms, corridors, lobbies, service areas, shafts, roof zones, and external areas. In repeated locations such as powder rooms, corridors, stores, and similar service areas, the MEP team had to describe the complete address of the location, which sometimes created confusion.
Root Cause
A complete project-wide location identification system was not established at the design and early coordination stage. Room names were shown on drawings, but unique room numbers, corridor numbers, zone codes, and location references were not consistently applied across architectural, MEP, QA/QC, planning, testing and commissioning, and handover documents.
Impact on Project
Lack of unique location numbering can cause confusion in work planning, progress tracking, inspection requests, snagging, testing and commissioning records, asset tagging, valve tagging, material installation, and future maintenance. The issue may appear minor during normal execution, but it can become serious for critical systems, concealed services, emergency response, troubleshooting, or facility management after handover.
Lesson Learned
Every room, corridor, lobby, shaft, plantroom, roof area, external zone, and service location within the project boundary must have a clear and unique identification name and number. Location identification is essential for traceability of MEP works, equipment, valves, access panels, inspection records, T&C data, asset registers, O&M manuals, and future maintenance activities.
Recommended Preventive Action
At the design and early construction stage, establish a complete location numbering system in coordination with architectural, MEP, QA/QC, planning, testing and commissioning, and facility management teams. The system may be numeric, alphabetical, alphanumeric, or based on a project MIS/coding system. No area within the project boundary should remain without identification. Use the same location codes consistently in drawings, shop drawings, inspection requests, progress reports, snag lists, T&C documents, asset registers, valve schedules, O&M manuals, and handover records. MEP item tag numbers should also be linked to the location code wherever practical.
